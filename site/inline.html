Pixel super-resolution

Each frame is integrated over the physical pixels; moving the source shifts the hologram by a fraction of a pixel. Shift-and-add interleaves the frames on a K× finer grid, then the usual reconstruction runs. This is the classic PSR method with known shifts, simpler than the differentiable joint optimisation of Chen & Lam (2025), which also estimates the shifts and the distance.

Reconstruction

µm

Direct reconstruction back-propagates the hologram to the sample plane. Its twin image appears as ghost fringes around the object. The iterative step (Gerchberg–Saxton with an object-plane constraint) removes most of it. The phase constraint assumes the sample delays light by 0 to π relative to the background; stronger objects wrap and need the absorbing constraint or a thinner sample.

How this microscope works

The pinhole makes a spherical wave. Light that passes the sample undisturbed is the reference; light scattered by the sample is the object wave. They interfere on the camera, and that intensity pattern is the hologram. The shadow of the sample is magnified by M = L / z₁, which is why no lens is needed.

Numerically, a point-source hologram is equivalent to a plane-wave hologram of the sample recorded at the distance z_eff = z₁(L − z₁)/L, with camera pixels shrunk by M (the Fresnel scaling theorem). This bench uses that equivalence with band-limited angular-spectrum propagation.

Coherence: a laser is spatially coherent, so a bigger pinhole does not blur the hologram. It changes the illumination: a narrower cone, less magnification, and for a large unfocused pinhole a near-field (Fresnel) pattern of the aperture itself. The reconstruction divides this known illumination out. A blur from the pinhole size would only appear with a partially coherent source such as an LED.

Why a uniform glass plate is invisible: it only adds the same phase everywhere, and the camera records intensity. Inline holography sees changes in optical path, such as edges, etched features, cells and particles. A plate becomes visible only where its thickness or index changes.
